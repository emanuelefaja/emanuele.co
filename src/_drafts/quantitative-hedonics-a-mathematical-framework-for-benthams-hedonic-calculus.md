Jeremy Bentham's hedonic calculus, proposed in the 19th century, aimed to numerically quantify the utility or felicific value of actions. The calculus considers seven factors - intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent. However, Bentham did not rigorously specify how to aggregate these factors into an overall measure of utility mathematically. This paper explores mathematical frameworks to model the calculation, considering additive, multiplicative, and hybrid formulations. A probabilistic and psychometric approach is proposed based on multi-attribute utility theory and modern quality of life measurement. The seven attributes are conceptualized as numerical value functions, incorporating probability weights for uncertainty. Different aggregation models are assessed against criteria like theoretical validity and computational accuracy. Results provide a robust mathematical formalization of the hedonic calculus, enabling the quantification of complex utility tradeoffs. This provides a foundation to apply Bentham’s utilitarianism empirically. The model balances prescriptiveness from moral philosophy with descriptiveness from behavioural science. Formalizing the calculus also highlights challenges like subjective experience versus objective functional forms. Implications for computational ethics and quantified utilitarianism are discussed.
