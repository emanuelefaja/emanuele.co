This essay explores the various themes in the film “The Beach” (2000), directed by Danny Boyle. It’s an adventure drama about an isolated community on a perfect beach. It is based on Alex Garland's 1996 novel of the same name and was shot on the Thai island of Ko Phi Phi Le.

Leonardo DiCaprio stars as the main protagonist, "Richard," a young American tourist. Interestingly, DiCaprio was hot off the massive success of The Titanic, which shot him to stardom. It became the world’s highest-earning box-office success, with $2.1B in receipts worldwide. DiCaprio noted, *"I have no connection with me during that whole Titanic phenomenon and what my face became around the world. I’ll never reach that state of popularity again, and I don't expect to. It's not something I'm going to try to achieve either.”*

The Beach received mixed to negative reviews, which is understandable considering that this is a strange film that does not easily fit into a category. However, the Beach was a commercial success, earning $180m against a budget of $35m.

The plot follows Richard, who travels to Thailand to experience *“something more beautiful, something more exciting, and yes, I admit, something more dangerous.”* Richard is given a map to a hidden, idyllic island by a strange man named Daffy in Bangkok, who then commits suicide. Richard then sets out to find the island with the help of two French travellers, Françoise and Étienne. After a treacherous journey, the trio finally reaches the island and discovers a lush paradise inhabited by a group of people, led by a woman called Sal, who have created their own society, free from the rules and restrictions of the outside world. It’s a *“beach resort for people who don't like beach resorts.”* However, as Richard becomes more integrated into the community, events conspire to challenge the group’s moral values and shatter their peaceful existence.

The film explores themes of travel, leisure, perfection, meaning, escapism, isolation, utopia, ethics, and the consequences of seeking out an escape from the real world.

As I recently rewatched this film, I was struck by how much my own perspective and experiences had changed since I previously watched it when I was just a teenager. The film significantly influenced me at the time, partly inspiring me to take my first trip to Vietnam and Cambodia, which turned out to be a major life decision. I ended up eventually moving to Cambodia and living there for eight years. I now write these words from Bali. So in some ways, I deeply understand the appeal of the film's message of escaping society and living a simpler life.

Re-watching the film made me question the desirability of the lifestyle it portrays. Would it lead to positive outcomes? I found it intriguing how the film remained the same, yet my perspective had changed from teenager to adult. Living and experiencing more has provided me with a deeper understanding of the film. I have been fortunate enough to have the opportunity to travel extensively, spending the past two years exploring different countries and cultures. This experience has allowed me to see the world from a different perspective and has given me a greater appreciation for the complexities and nuances of different societies. As I have encountered various challenges ([including being kidnapped in Colombia](/a-gun-to-my-head/)) and different ways of life, I have realised that travelling and changing locations will not solve one's inner problems. Wherever you go, [you must bring yourself with you](/the-curse-of-the-traveller/).

The simple, idyllic existence depicted on the beach is not necessarily desirable. This realization has influenced my thoughts on the film and has shaped my understanding of its themes. I want to use a philosophical lens to critically analyze some of the moral and ethical implications of the actions depicted in the film.

Let’s see if any life lessons can be discovered!

Let's start by asking a rather fundamental question: why travel? What motivated Richard to go to the other side of the world?

There are many different reasons why people might choose to travel. Some people travel to explore new cultures and ways of life, while others travel in search of new experiences or to learn new things. Still, others travel to escape their daily routines or to relax and recharge. From a philosophical perspective, travel can be seen as a method for broadening one's horizons and expanding one's understanding of the world. It can also be used to challenge one's beliefs and assumptions and discover new perspectives on life and the world. Ultimately, the reasons for travel are likely to be as varied as those who undertake it and may include a combination of personal, cultural, and existential considerations.

> The world is a book and those who do not travel read only one page.
>
> **St. Augustine**

However, the film is critical of mass commercial travel, with Richard stating, *"In one moment, l understood more clearly than ever why we were so special, why we kept our secret. Because if we didn’t, sooner or later, they'd turn it into this. Cancers. Parasites. Eating up the whole fuckin' world."*

And I think this has gotten significantly worse since the film was released.

The proliferation of social media has significantly impacted how people travel and experience new places. In popular destinations like Bali, for example, tourists flock to the same few landmarks and attractions that have become Instagram-famous, such as waterfalls and rice fields. I have noticed this as I write these words from Ubud, the cultural centre of Bali. There are a few famous walks full of tourists, and barely ten minutes away, other walks are equally as beautiful but deserted.

This phenomenon, which I refer to as "travel pornography," creates a distorted and superficial view of a destination. Tourists are often unaware that many other, lesser-known places may be just as beautiful and authentic but lack the commercial appeal and perfect Instagrammable quality of the more popular sites. as a result; they may miss out on truly unique and meaningful experiences in favour of seeking out the perfect photo opportunity. In a rush to capture the perfect shot, they contribute to overcrowding and the degradation of the places they try to enjoy.

And I find that this is one thing that is missing in almost all places: a sense of adventure. I am not talking about the fact that things need to be dangerous, but that every place on the planet is rushing to become comfortable and sanitized, so tourists can flock to take the same photos to ensure that their Instagram profile is envious.

This obsession with the "lonely photographer in the middle of nowhere" aesthetic has led to a paradox in contemporary travel culture. On the one hand, people are drawn to the idea of escaping civilization and finding solitude in remote and exotic locations. on the other hand, they are unwilling to *actually* go to these places and experience the realities of life in the "middle of nowhere." Instead, they seek out the illusion of isolation and authenticity, but only within the confines of a carefully curated and commercialized travel experience. This trend highlights the superficiality of contemporary travel culture and the dangers of prioritizing social media validation over genuine exploration and connection with a place.

My experience of travelling the world is that everything is starting to look like a copy of a copy of a copy in many places. I sit in a coffee shop, and rarely can I tell which country I am in. The same food, avocado toast everywhere, cappuccinos, and so on. Some countries and cultures resist this, Italy, parts of Europe, Morocco, and parts of Indonesia, but on the whole, there are fewer and fewer reasons to travel the world and try to find new things — because you now find the same things everywhere.

However, in the film, they perhaps took this criticism too far. They constantly compared the peaceful and isolated community to the drunk and whoring foreign tourists in Thailand, while society is much broader and better than that. Destinations such as Thailand, Cambodia, and Vietnam draw many fascinating people. Yet, they also attract sex tourists and individuals of the lowest calibre who failed to make it in their home countries and chose to start anew.

One of the main reasons why people travel is that it is a form of escapism. In a new place, you can be a new person. Nobody knows you, and nobody cares what you do. You instantly wash away your sins, and you can break free from your daily routine. This is a compelling experience for those who lack meaning in their everyday lives. Perhaps they work jobs they hate so they can buy shit they don't need. I don't know what this is like, but I can't imagine it is pleasant waking up day after day to do something that you have no passion for. It is essential for individuals to [comprehend the two types of work in life](/the-two-types-of-work/), so they can discover true autotelic work that brings them satisfaction.

However, there is significant criticism of this idea that does not often get considered in the modern world. We automatically equate more travel with a better life. But travelling and seeing new places does not inherently bring value or happiness to one's life, no matter how much those who are passionate about travel may try to convince others. There is no reward at the end of life for visiting the most places. Seneca wrote a letter titled "On Travel as a Cure for Discontent", and it feels like it could have been written yesterday:

> Do you suppose that you alone have had this experience? Are you surprised, as if it were a novelty, that after such long travel and so many changes of scene you have not been able to shake off the gloom and heaviness of your mind? You need a change of soul rather than a change of climate. Though you may cross vast spaces of sea...your faults will follow you whithersoever you travel. Socrates made the same remark to one who complained; he said: "Why do you wonder that globe-trotting does not help you, seeing that you always take yourself with you? The reason which set you wandering is ever at your heels." What pleasure is there in seeing new lands? Or in surveying cities and spots of interest? All your bustle is useless. Do you ask why such flight does not help you? It is because you flee along with yourself. You must lay aside the burdens of the mind; until you do this, no place will satisfy you.

And this is perfectly encapsulated in the storyline of The Beach. They ran away from society, but they couldn't run away from themselves, and trouble inevitably followed. The shark was used as a motif in the movie for the unknown problems in life that lurk beneath the surface unseen.

So Richard escapes society to this paradise but ends up spending his time playing on his Nintendo Gameboy. Is this any better than just staying in his parent's basement back in America? Perhaps he is not very different to the people he left behind, or from the version of himself that he thought he had left behind. There is no real grand philosophical statement, no finding anything new, just leisure for the sake of leisure.

He says to his parents, “*I like it out here, you know. Things are different.”* Do people just want something different from their home? Does our brain crave novelty? Is it driven by dopamine-induced urges to explore? This comes from a choice of paying attention. Even our own backyard or room can become a travel experience if we focus enough. It is all about how much attention we give.
