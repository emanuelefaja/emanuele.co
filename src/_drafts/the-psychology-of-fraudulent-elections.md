I spent the majority of my twenties in Cambodia, and I experienced a few elections during that time.

Cambodia has been under the leadership of Prime Minister Hun Sen for over three decades. Hun Sen, a former Khmer Rouge commander, has been dominant in Cambodian politics since the 1980s. His rule, often characterised by authoritarian tendencies, has been marked by political stability, economic growth, and a significant reduction in poverty. I've seen first-hand the remarkable changes in Phnom Penh in just ten years.

However, it has also been marred by allegations of human rights abuses, corruption, electoral fraud, and restrictions on the media. Stories about people being arrested for Facebook posts\[mfn\][Man arrested on wedding day for criticising government on Facebook](https://www.phnompenhpost.com/national/man-arrested-wedding-day-criticising-government-facebook)\[/mfn\]

I remember the last edition of the Cambodia Daily, which I framed and hung on the wall in my apartment.

![](/media/2023/08/cambodia-daily-1024x1024.jpeg)

I always found it strange that living in Cambodia, day to day, did not feel that much different from living in Europe. Of course, I am a foreigner with little invested in the long-term development of the country. I can pack up and move out at any time; it is not my homeland, where I have strong family ties. However, there are significant differences outside the expat bubble, especially when you run up against the government.

There are also plenty of other examples of media shutdowns or take-overs in Cambodia, including The Phnom Penh Post, another English-language newspaper, which was sold to a Malaysian investor with reported ties to Hun Sen in 2018 \[mfn\][Newspaper takeover is 'staggering blow' to Cambodia's free press](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2018/may/06/newspaper-takeover-staggering-blow-cambodia-free-press-phnom-penh-post)\[/mfn\]. This takeover led to a mass exodus of senior staff members, who cited concerns over the newspaper's editorial independence. The Phnom Penh Post had been one of the few remaining independent voices in Cambodia's media landscape, and its takeover was seen as a significant blow to press freedom in the country.

In 2017, Radio Free Asia, a US-funded news organization known for its critical coverage of the Cambodian government, was forced to close its in-country operations. The government accused it of failing to register with the tax department, a charge that Radio Free Asia denied. This closure was seen as part of a broader crackdown on independent media and civil society in the lead-up to the 2018 general elections.

Several local radio stations that relayed broadcasts from Radio Free Asia and Voice of America, another US-funded news organization, were also shut down. These radio stations played a crucial role in providing independent news to Cambodia's rural population, many of whom do not have access to the internet and rely on radio for information.

Furthermore, several independent Khmer-language newspapers were closed or taken over. Prachatai, a newspaper known for its critical coverage of the government, was forced to shut down. Moneaksekar Khmer, a newspaper affiliated with the opposition party, was also closed after its editor was charged with defamation.

These developments have significantly altered the media landscape in Cambodia, limiting the number of independent voices and increasing the government's control over information. Critics argue that this has created an environment of self-censorship, where journalists and media outlets hesitate to criticize the government for fear of reprisals. This has further consolidated the CPP's hold on power and limited the space for democratic debate and dissent.

Recently, I engaged in a conversation about the latest developments in Cambodian politics, particularly the surprising resignation of Hun Sen, a move that marked the end of his four-decade-long rule\[mfn\][Cambodia's Hun Sen to resign after four decades and appoint son as PM](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-66309249). In a significant political shift, Hun Sen passed the baton of leadership to his son, adding a new dimension to the power dynamics in the country.

This transition has led to an intriguing scenario in which Cambodia now has two distinct power lineages—the royal family and the Hun Sen family. Under King Norodom Sihamoni, the royal family holds a constitutional but largely symbolic role, while the Hun Sen family, with its deep-rooted influence in politics and the military, is now at the helm of governance.

In some ways, I found this quite funny.

They hold elections to have a veneer of the democratic process, only for then Hun Sen to resign and give power to his son, which is an obviously non-democratic move! This succession plan, akin to a monarchy, starkly contrasts with the democratic ideals that elections should uphold.

The irony of the situation is not lost on observers \[mfn\][Certain of election victory, Cambodia’s Hun Sen prepares to hand power to son](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/jul/21/cambodia-hun-sen-certain-election-victory-hand-power-son-hun-manet)\[/mfn\]. The facade of democracy is maintained through the conduct of elections, which are marred by allegations of irregularities. Then, in an overtly non-democratic move, Hun Sen resigns and hands over power to his son, bypassing any electoral process.

Cambodia is not alone in this phenomenon. Throughout history, numerous totalitarian regimes have held elections that serve more as a symbolic gesture rather than a genuine democratic process. Some examples include:

**1\. North Korea:** The Democratic People's Republic of Korea holds elections every five years. However, these elections are largely seen as a formality, as there is typically only one candidate on the ballot for each district, all of whom are members of the ruling Workers' Party of Korea or its allies.

**2\. Soviet Union:** During the Soviet Union era, elections were regularly held. However, these were largely ceremonial as all candidates were members of the Communist Party, and there was no genuine opposition. 

**3\. Nazi Germany:** Adolf Hitler, after coming to power, held a series of plebiscites and elections to legitimize his rule. However, these were conducted in an atmosphere of intimidation and manipulation, with the Nazi Party being the only party allowed to campaign.

**4\. Iran:** While Iran holds regular elections, the Guardian Council, an unelected body, can disqualify candidates. This process has often been criticized for limiting the pool of candidates to those loyal to the Supreme Leader and the ruling establishment.

**5\. Zimbabwe:** Under Robert Mugabe's rule, Zimbabwe held regular elections. However, these were often marred by violence, intimidation, and allegations of vote-rigging, allowing Mugabe to maintain his grip on power for several decades.

These examples illustrate that the mere conduct of elections does not guarantee democracy. In many totalitarian regimes, elections legitimise the ruling powers and maintain a veneer of democratic governance.

But I want to dive deeper and understand why the authorities bother running elections. You can speak to any taxi driver in any totalitarian country, and they will tell you that the election process is a running joke, yet the authorities continue to run their fake elections—it's intriguing!

One obvious reason is that you can avoid international sanctions if you hold elections. I am writing these words from Afghanistan, where the Taliban took power a few years ago by force, and the economy is crippled by international sanctions such as the freezing of more than $7 billion in Afghan government reserves held in the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. This was done to deny the Taliban access to these funds.

Elections, even those conducted under obviously fraudulent conditions, are a strategic tool for authoritarian regimes to exercise control over the populace and manage opposition. During the election period, opposition parties become more active and visible, allowing the ruling powers to closely monitor their activities, strategies, and networks. Even a manipulated voting process can offer valuable insights into public sentiment.

For example, unexpected support for an opposition party in a certain region could indicate a pocket of dissent, allowing the ruling powers to target their policies or repressive measures more effectively. Elections also help identify potential threats, such as emerging opposition leaders or regions with strong anti-government sentiment.

Once these threats are identified, the regime can neutralize them through various means such as arrests, intimidation, or co-optation. By allowing the opposition to participate in a rigged election, the regime creates an illusion of political pluralism, appeasing dissent and maintaining control. Even if the public is aware of the election's fraudulent nature, voting can instil a sense of participation and consent, reducing the likelihood of rebellion. In this way, fraudulent elections serve as a multifaceted strategy for authoritarian regimes to consolidate power, manage opposition, and maintain a semblance of a democratic process.
