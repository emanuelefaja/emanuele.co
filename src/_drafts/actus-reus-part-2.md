Actus Reus (AR) can further be subdivided into three possible types of acts.

1.  **Commission —** This is a voluntary act by D that was criminal.
2.  **Omission —** This is something that D *didn’t* do.
3.  **Possession —** This is when D is in possession of an illegal item (i.e. a weapon or drugs).

I find the second one particularly strange because *not* doing something can also be part of AR, which, let’s remember stands for “Guilty Act”.

We have essentially discussed acts of commission in part 1, indirectly via our discussion on tests of factual and legal causation. However, those tests apply to all acts of commission *and* acts of omission.

So let’s dive directly into acts of omission. This is when a defendant (D) fails to engage in a necessary *bodily movement* resulting in injury to V.

This makes sense, and was explored in an interesting manner in one of the short stories that make up the book *I, Robot. by Issac Asimov*.

In the future worlds in the writings of Asimov, there are the three laws of robotics:

1.  The first law is that a robot shall not harm a human, or by inaction allow a human to come to harm.
2.  The second law is that a robot shall obey any instruction given to it by a human.
3.  The third law is that a robot shall avoid actions or situations that could cause it to come to harm itself.

And we can see, that the first law of robotics directly links to our discussion of D causing V harm both by action and inaction.

In one of the short stories, there is a unique robot created that has a modified first law of robotics that removes the inaction clause in the law. This enables the robot to work alongside humans in industrial settings where some harm may come to the humans, but the harm is acceptable to the humans themselves. Before this modification, the robot in question would forcibly remove the humans from the scene.

Eventually, this robot goes missing, and…an interesting story happens! It is not lost on the characters that this robot could be very dangerous because one can imagine lots of scenarios where it could injure a human without breaking that first law of robotics.

Interestingly, in common law, there is no requirement to help out a stranger or to be a “Good Samaritan”. You can legally watch a stranger drowning and decide to do nothing. There are various exceptions to this, but we will get into that later.

I’ll let the fountain of knowledge provide an overview of the parable of the Good Samaritan:

> The parable of the Good Samaritan is told by Jesus  in the Gospel of Luke. It is about a traveler (implicitly understood to be Jewish) who is stripped of clothing, beaten, and left half dead alongside the road. First, a Jewish priest and then a Levite come by, but both avoid the man. Finally, a Samaritan happens upon the traveler. Although Samaritans and Jews despised each other, the Samaritan helps the injured man. Jesus is described as telling the parable in response to a provocative question from a lawyer, "And who is my neighbor?", in the context of the Great Commandment. The conclusion is that the neighbor figure in the parable is the one who shows mercy to their fellow man.

The underlining idea here is that a moral duty to act does not equate to a legal duty. This is because we view a commission as worse than an omission, even if the result is the same. In fact, sometimes *not* doing something has a worse result, but we still prefer inaction to action. This is a cognitive bias that most of us have, but something that psychopaths do not struggle with.

This was explored quite well in a book that I've been reading recently:

> A brilliant transplant surgeon has five patients. Each of the patients is in need of a different organ, and each of them will die without that organ. While performing the checkup, the doctor discovers that the young man’s organs are compatible with all five of his dying patients. G. Williams in ‘Criminal Omissions—The Conventional View’ (1991) argues this point:

> If there is no fundamental moral distinction between killing and letting die (in breach of duty), it is a fact that has been missed by members of the medical profession, who see a great difference between the two. Whereas killing your patient is absolutely taboo, according to the present law and official medical ethics, letting your patient die is qualifiedly permissible, namely when the patient is dying and there is no point in continuing his agony.The arguments for this conventional philosophy may be briefly stated. … First, society’s most urgent task is the repression of active wrongdoing. Bringing the ignorant or lethargic up to scratch is very much a secondary endeavour, for which the criminal process is not necessarily the best suited. Secondly, our attitudes to wrongful action and wrongful inaction differ. There may be instances where our blood boils at the same temperature on account of both, but these cases are very exceptional. The only likely instance that comes to my mind is that of parents who are charged with killing their baby (i) by smothering it or (ii) by starving it to death. In this instance we are likely to feel more angry and sad about the slow starvation (an omission) than about the comparatively merciful infliction of death with a pillow. But on other occasions we almost always perceive a moral distinction between (for example) killing a person and failing to save his life (the former being the worse); and similarly between other acts and corresponding omissions. The moral distinction … reflects differences in our psychological approach to our own acts and omissions. We have much stronger inhibitions against active wrongdoing than against wrongfully omitting. … Also, a requirement to do something presupposes the ability to do it (the physical ability, and often the financial and educational ability as well), whereas almost everyone has the ability to refrain from ordinary physical acts. Thirdly, serious crimes of commission can usually be formulated merely stating the forbidden conduct, but laws creating crimes of omission are rarely directed against the whole world. They are intended to operate only against particular classes of person (and sometimes only for the protection of particular classes), in which case these persons must be singled out in the statement of the crime. To take an example: the courts can, in theory, punish everyone … who knowingly kills, but they cannot punish everyone who fails to save life, without some minimum specification of whose lives are to be saved. I cannot be made criminally responsible when I knowingly fail to save (and do not even try to save) the lives of unfortunate inhabitants of the Ganges delta who are drowned in floods; yet I could do something to help them by selling my house and giving the money to a suitable charity.The argument against treating omissions in the same way as positive acts does not go to the extent of saying that omissions running contrary to the public interest should never be punishable. Those who oppose seat belt legislation … do so on the ground that it unjustifiably restricts liberty, not on the ground that it wrongly punishes omissions. The legislation forbids one to drive in a car without belting up, and the forbidden conduct is a hybrid act/omission, which is legally classified as an act, not an omission.

The big problem with omission, is that when you omit to take an action, you may well not know the outcome. So then you may not know that your failure to act is criminal until *after* the event. This, then will always fail the Mens Rea (MR) part of the equation for criminal liability.

And where do we stop? You not selling all your possessions and donating money to an anti-malaria charity. This is likely killing one person for every $3,000 you could donate, but that does not make you legally liable for those deaths. It does not even morally liable — we do not *yet* have expectations that everyone in economically developed countries needs to sell everything to help the rest of the world.

## When do you have a duty to act?

For the purpose of criminal liability, there are five reasons that someone *must* act.

1\. **A statute requires a person to act in a certain way.**

This is quite a clear one, and something that we are subject to every day. Let me provide a few examples.

The Road Traffic Act 1988 states that:

> *Failing to provide a breath test when driving or in charge of a motor vehicle, under the influence of alcohol or after commission of a traffic offence, and without reasonable excuse (s6(4));Failing to produce driving licence to constable or to state date of birth (s164(6)).*

The Education Act 1996 states that:

> *If a child of compulsory school age who is a registered pupil at a school fails to attend regularly at the school, their parent is guilty of an offence (s444(1)).*

The Terrorism Act 2000 states that:

> *Where a person believes or suspects that someone else has committed certain terrorist offences, that person commits an offence if they do not disclose to a constable as soon as is reasonably practicable (1) their belief or suspicion, and (2) the information on which it is based (s19).*

So, there are laws that require us to act in a certain manner in certain situations, and we commit a crime if we do not act. These are conduct crimes, and do not necessarily require harm to occur to a victim.

**2\. A contract requires a person to act in a certain way.**

This normally occurs when someone is employed and thus has an employment contract and assume responsibility for third parties, such as the public. If D fails to act in his professional capacity and harms a member of the public, he may be liable of a crime by omission.

Criminal law (in England) differs here from Contract Law where the doctrine of privity of contract prevents anyone who is not a party to the contract from enforcing it.

In ***R v Pittwood*** (1902) 19 TLR 37, Taunton Assizes:

> A gatekeeper on the Somerset and Dorset Railway was under a contractual duty to keep the crossing gates closed whenever a train was passing from 7.00 am to 7.00 pm. He forgot to shut the gates one afternoon and a passing train struck a hay cart. One man was killed and another was seriously injured. D argued that his responsibility was to his employers and not to the public. He was held guilty of manslaughter on the grounds of gross and criminal negligence and an *assumption* of responsibility to the public. His duty was not simply to his employers.  In *Pittwood*, D’s contractual duty to protect the public was specifically owed to his employers but this did not prevent conviction.

One thing that I did consider about this type of contractual obligation, is the issue of risk to the person under contract. So, for instance, if a lifeguard fails to save a child's life at sea because they didn't want to enter the water due to the weather, at what point do they stop being criminally liable by omission? Obviously if there was 100% chance of death during the rescue, then the lifeguard is not liable because there is nothing he could have done. But, life hardly ever provides clear-cut 100% situations.

This is echoed in the Social Responsibility view of "The Good Samaritan".

> The social responsibility view of omissions liability grows out of a communitarian social philosophy which stresses the necessary interrelationship between individual behaviour and collective goods. Individuals need others, or the actions of others, for a wide variety of tasks which assist each one of us to maximise the pursuit of our personal goals. A community or society may be regarded as a network of relationships which support one another by direct and indirect means. But the community also consists of individuals, each having certain basic rights (such as the right to life). It is therefore strongly arguable that each individual life should be valued both intrinsically and for its contribution (or potential contribution) to the community. It follows that there is a good case for encouraging co-operation at the minimal level of the duty to assist persons in peril, **so long as the assistance does not endanger the person rendering it**, and a case may be made for reinforcing this duty by the criminal sanction. … The argument does not rest on a simple utilitarian calculation of benefits, ensuring a net saving of lives with comparatively little inconvenience to other members of society. Nor does it rest on the prediction that both respect for the law and the level of social co-operation will be improved if the law encourages morally desirable conduct, although those would be beneficial consequences. The foundation of the argument is that a level of social co-operation and social responsibility is both good and necessary for the realisation of individual autonomy

**3\. Some special status relationship exists that creates a duty to act in a certain way**.

**4\. A voluntary assumption of care creates a duty to act in a certain way.**

**5\. The individual created the risk.**

In conclusion with regard to omission, one cannot be criminally liable for an omission unless a duty of care is owed or there is a statute mandating certain behaviour.
