I have been thinking lately that there is a strong correlation between Democracy and education. I want to explore this today, starting from the question of why we even have a government, considering the history of the democratic process, and then to common criticisms, and finally, a discussion on education requirements for voting.

## Why Have Government?

Why have government in the first place? This question almost seems crazy to ask, *of course* we need government.

I think, we will reach the same answer that I reached in [my essay on invisible management,](/good-management-in-invisible/) that we have government because things are better with government than without, but that does not tell us much about the overall quality of government.

There are many Lybians and Iraqis who preferred the relative safety and iron rule of Ghadafi and Sadam Hussein respectively, and when I travelled to Afghanistan, there were even some people I met there who preferred the rule of the Taliban, with the drastic improvement in safety, than the free rule of the American-led regime with the instability and lack of personal safety. This does not mean that these governments were any good, it just means that even those governments are better than total anarchy, where nobody is there to help you when you need it.

It is clear that democracy is flawed; even a cursory glance at the strange incentive structures it creates tells us that.

None of these criticisms are new, Plato covered an interesting one in his work *The Republic:*

*Imagine a scene where a doctor and a candy seller are trying to win the favour of a group of children. The doctor, who knows what is best for the children's health, may prescribe bitter medicines and forbid them from eating too many sweets. On the other hand, the candy seller will try to persuade the children by offering them all kinds of candies and sweets.*

Democracy, by its very nature, is a system that relies on the collective decision-making of the people. While this is often seen as a strength, as it gives voice to the desires and needs of the populace, it also raises important questions about the priorities and long-term consequences of democratic choices. One such question is whether democracy, in its pursuit of popular approval, tends to prioritize short-term gratification over the long-term necessities and values that are essential for a society's sustained well-being and progress.

In many democratic societies, political leaders and parties often make promises of increased social spending, lower taxes, and policies designed to please as many people as possible. While these promises may be attractive to voters in the short term, they can lead to unintended consequences and undermine the long-term stability and prosperity of a nation. By focusing on immediate gratification rather than long-term planning and investment, democracies risk creating unsustainable economic policies, neglecting critical infrastructure, and failing to address complex, long-term challenges such as climate change, social inequality, and demographic shifts.

Moreover, the tendency to prioritize short-term gratification in democratic decision-making can create a *"race to the bottom"* in which political leaders compete to offer the most appealing promises, regardless of their feasibility or long-term impact.

And because we tend to elect leaders in 4 year cycles, there is always a tendency to kick the can down the road, to let another leadership team handle the problems caused. The incentives are necessarily short term.

This can lead to a situation where necessary but unpopular decisions are avoided, and important values and principles are compromised in pursuing electoral success.

In essence, the question is whether this approach to democratic governance is a misguided way to play the game of life and government. While the intentions behind promises of increased spending and lower taxes may be good, the reality is that they often lead to outcomes that leave everyone worse off in the long run. By prioritizing short-term gratification over long-term necessities and values, democracies risk undermining the very foundations of their success and stability.

## History of Democracy.

Democracy, a system of government in which the people hold the power to govern, has its roots in ancient civilizations. The concept of involving the public in decision-making processes can be traced back to various societies, such as ancient Mesopotamia, India, and the Phoenician city-states. However, these early examples of proto-democratic practices were often limited in scope and existed alongside other forms of government.

It was in ancient Greece, particularly in Athens during the 5th century BCE, that democracy as we understand it today first flourished. The Athenian system of direct democracy was a groundbreaking experiment in which citizens actively participated in the legislative, judicial, and executive functions of government. Despite its limitations and short-lived nature, the Athenian democracy served as a powerful example of the potential for self-governance and the rule of the people.

Following the decline of Athenian democracy, the idea of democracy receded into the background for many centuries. However, it was never entirely lost, as democratic principles and philosophies were preserved through the works of various thinkers and in some limited forms of representative governance.

It was during the Age of Enlightenment in the 17th and 18th centuries that democratic ideas were rediscovered and gained new prominence. The intellectual movement of this era challenged the prevailing notions of absolute monarchy and divine right, paving the way for the establishment of modern democratic republics in the wake of the American and French Revolutions.

The rediscovery and resurgence of democracy in the modern era demonstrate the enduring appeal and resilience of this ancient idea. The fact that democratic principles have stood the test of time, surviving and adapting through centuries of political, social, and economic change, is a testament to their fundamental strength and importance.

As we reflect on the history of democracy, from its ancient roots to its modern incarnations, we are reminded that the ideas that have endured the longest are often the most valuable. The longevity and adaptability of democracy suggest that it is a system of government that has been stress-tested over millennia, making it worthy of our respect and commitment to its continued development and improvement.

**Pre-Greek Democracy.**

Before delving into the well-known example of ancient Greek democracy, it is important to acknowledge the existence of proto-democratic or quasi-democratic practices in other ancient civilizations. While these early forms of public participation in decision-making were often limited and not as well-documented as the Athenian model, they nonetheless provide valuable insights into the early development of democratic ideas.

In ancient Mesopotamia, some city-states, such as Uruk and Lagash, had forms of popular assemblies that participated in decision-making alongside the ruler. The Code of Hammurabi, a Babylonian legal code, also suggests the existence of some form of public participation in the legal process. These examples indicate that the concept of involving the people in governance was not entirely foreign to Mesopotamian societies.

Similarly, in ancient India, the Vedas, which are ancient Hindu scriptures, mention the concepts of "sabha" and "samiti," referring to assemblies of the people that discussed important issues. Some of the republics mentioned in ancient Indian texts, such as the Licchavis and the Vajjis, are believed to have had elements of participatory decision-making, suggesting that democratic ideas were not confined to the Western world.

The Phoenician city-states, such as Tyre and Sidon, also had a form of government that included a king, a council of elders, and a popular assembly. Although the extent of the popular assembly's power is not entirely clear, its existence points to a recognition of the importance of public participation in governance.

In ancient Sumer, city-states had a form of government that included a king and a council of elders. Some scholars argue that the council of elders represented a form of proto-democracy, as they were chosen from among the citizens, hinting at the early seeds of representative governance.

It is crucial to recognize that these examples do not represent fully developed democratic systems like the one that would later emerge in ancient Athens. They often coexisted with monarchical or oligarchic elements and had limited participation based on factors such as gender, social class, or lineage. Nonetheless, they demonstrate that the idea of involving the public in decision-making processes had roots in various ancient civilizations, setting the stage for the more advanced and well-documented democratic experiments to come.

By acknowledging the pre-Greek proto-democratic practices, we gain a more comprehensive understanding of the long and diverse history of democracy. These early examples remind us that the desire for self-governance and public participation in decision-making is a universal human aspiration that has manifested in different forms throughout history, laying the groundwork for the development of more sophisticated and inclusive democratic systems in the centuries that followed.

### Greek Democracy.

The ancient Greeks, particularly in the city-state of Athens, are widely credited with developing the first known system of direct democracy. This innovative form of government emerged in the 5th century BCE and lasted for nearly two centuries, leaving an indelible mark on the history of political thought and practice.

Before the rise of democracy, Athens was ruled by a series of kings and aristocrats. In the 7th century BCE, Athens was governed by a small group of wealthy landowners known as the aristocracy. This period was marked by social and political unrest, as the majority of the population, consisting of small farmers and artisans, was often subjected to debt slavery and oppression by the aristocratic class.

The situation came to a head during the reign of the tyrant Draco, who introduced a harsh legal code in 621 BCE that prescribed severe punishments, including death, for minor offenses. The code was seen as excessively brutal and further exacerbated the tensions between the aristocracy and the common people.

In 594 BCE, Solon, an Athenian statesman and lawgiver, was appointed to reform the political and legal system. Solon introduced a series of reforms aimed at alleviating the plight of the poor and reducing the power of the aristocracy. He abolished debt slavery, reformed the legal code, and divided the Athenian population into four classes based on their wealth, granting political rights proportional to their economic status. While Solon's reforms did not establish a full democracy, they laid the groundwork for future democratic developments by expanding political participation and reducing the concentration of power in the hands of the aristocracy.

The reign of the tyrant Peisistratos and his sons in the 6th century BCE, while not democratic, saw the implementation of policies that further weakened the aristocracy and strengthened the position of the common people. Peisistratos promoted the cultivation of olive trees, which became a key source of wealth for small farmers, and sponsored religious festivals and public works that benefited the general population.

The final push towards democracy came in 508 BCE, when Cleisthenes, an Athenian noble, introduced a series of reforms that established the basic institutions of the Athenian democratic system. Cleisthenes reorganized the Athenian population into ten tribes, each of which elected fifty members to the Boule, the council of five hundred. He also introduced the practice of ostracism, which allowed the Athenian people to banish politicians deemed dangerous to the democratic order.

The reforms of Cleisthenes marked the birth of the Athenian democracy, which reached its zenith in the 5th century BCE under the leadership of Pericles. During this period, known as the Golden Age of Athens, the city-state experienced a remarkable flourishing of political participation, artistic creativity, and intellectual inquiry.

The emergence of democracy in Athens was thus the result of a gradual process of political and social reform, driven by the demands of the common people for greater equality and participation in governance. The reforms of Solon and Cleisthenes, in particular, were crucial in laying the institutional foundations of the Athenian democratic system, which would go on to become one of the most significant and influential political experiments in human history.

The ancient Greeks, particularly in the city-state of Athens, are widely credited with developing the first known system of direct democracy. This innovative form of government emerged in the 5th century BCE and lasted for nearly two centuries, leaving an indelible mark on the history of political thought and practice.

The Athenian democracy was a remarkable experiment in direct participation, where citizens actively engaged in the legislative, judicial, and executive functions of government. The Athenian system was characterized by the Ekklesia, the popular assembly open to all male citizens over the age of 20, which met regularly to debate and vote on important issues, such as laws, foreign policy, and public expenditure.

Another key institution of Athenian democracy was the Boule, a council of 500 citizens chosen by lot to serve for a year. The Boule was responsible for setting the agenda for the Ekklesia and overseeing the day-to-day administration of the city-state. The use of sortition, or random selection, in choosing the members of the Boule was meant to ensure that all citizens had an equal chance of participating in governance.

The Athenian legal system was also a crucial component of its democracy. Citizens served as jurors in the law courts, hearing cases and delivering verdicts. This direct participation in the judicial process was seen as a fundamental right and responsibility of citizenship.

However, it is important to recognize that the Athenian democracy had its limitations. Citizenship and political participation were restricted to adult male citizens, excluding women, slaves, and foreigners, who made up a significant portion of the population. This exclusivity demonstrates that while the Athenian system was groundbreaking in its time, it did not fully embody the principles of equality and inclusivity that are central to modern understandings of democracy.

Despite its limitations, the Athenian democracy was a remarkable achievement that showcased the potential for self-governance and the rule of the people. The ideas and practices developed in Athens, such as the importance of public participation, the rule of law, and the accountability of leaders to the people, have had a lasting impact on the development of democratic thought and practice throughout history.

The legacy of Athenian democracy has inspired countless political thinkers, revolutionaries, and reformers in the centuries that followed, serving as a model and a point of reference for those seeking to establish more representative and participatory forms of government. While the specific institutions and practices of Athenian democracy may not be directly applicable to modern societies, the fundamental principles it embodied continue to shape our understanding of what democracy is and what it can be.
