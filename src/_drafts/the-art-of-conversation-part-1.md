The conversations we have shape the relationships we build, the lives we lead, and the world we create. The ability to hold a great conversation is *crucial* to flourishing in life. It can help us become deeper and more mindful individuals, persuade others of our point of view, and effortlessly make new connections anywhere we go. Being a great conversationalist can create a web of personal connections that pull you through life, ensuring that everyone you meet ends up on your team.

But the importance of conversation goes beyond the personal level. Sam Harris succinctly said: *“We have a choice. We have two options as human beings. We have a choice between conversation and war. That's it. Conversation and violence”.* So the ability to have a conversation, to take two different opinions and reach a compromise, to take someone's opinion and convince them of our argument, or — perhaps more importantly — to have a conversation and change our mind based on a deep understanding of the arguments being made, *is the only thing between us and World War 3.*

> I know not with what weapons World War III will be fought, but World War IV will be fought with sticks and stones.
>
> Einstein

So, conversation is the only thing standing between where we are now and civilization slipping back to the stone age.

I was fortunate enough to realize that I was a terrible conversationalist at some point in my mid-20s. I used to feel a strange sense of pride that most people would bore me, not realizing that if you find everyone boring, it actually means that *you* are the boring person. Instead of listening attentively, I was only waiting my turn to speak. Any story that someone would tell, I would give an even more impressive anecdote, as if having a conversation was a battle or competition.

At one point, I found myself living in the beautiful capital of Vietnam, Hanoi, spending most of my time walking around the lakes with a lot of spare time on my hands. I came across a second-hand copy of the book How to Win Friends and Influence People by Dale Carnegie, and one sentence gave me the *aha* moment: *"To be interesting, be interested."* I realized at that moment that I had a completely skewed understanding of what a conversation is and how to be a great conversationalist. Since then, I have made a concerted effort to engage in conversations with many people, and I have discovered the profound joy of connecting with others through authentic discussion.

I learned that the secret to being an engaging conversationalist is developing a genuine curiosity about people and what they share. By listening without judgment, following up with thoughtful questions, and reciprocating disclosure, I built rapport and trust, gained access to meaningful discussion topics, and formed mutually rewarding bonds with diverse individuals I encountered.

And most importantly, I learned that having great conversations with almost anyone is possible. It is a technique that can be learned, that's all. Through practice, conversing went from an awkward struggle to an improvisational art form and avenue for adventure. I have spent the last few years travelling full-time. I have challenged myself to strike up conversations with strangers in coffee shops, parks, and airports, which has led to a multitude of rich interactions and chance encounters that expanded my mind and reshaped my perspectives. Colleagues, friends, and sometimes even new acquaintances shared intimate details of their lives with me because they needed someone who would truly listen to them. I realized conversation has a power greater than any other activity for human flourishing and progress.

But the modern world is not helping us have great conversations. We're spending more and more time interacting via screens and messages than face-to-face and with our voices. Social media has been the largest uncontrolled psychological experiment that humanity has ever launched, and it is quite literally changing the way our brains work and how we process information.

The results are in, and it doesn't look good.

Suicide is on the rise, and many people, especially (and ironically) in dense urban centres, report a heightened sense of isolation.

Surrounded by people, and yet they are truly, fully alone.

Many people cannot hold an entire conversation without checking their phones multiple times, and we have all been to restaurants and seen an entire table that is not speaking, but the glow of their smartphone screens lights up their faces. In these cases, I always wonder why they are out in a restaurant in the first place.

> **We’ve created a world in which online connection has become primary. Especially for younger generations. And yet, in that world, anytime two people connect, the only way it’s financed is through a sneaky third person who’s paying to manipulate those two people. So we’ve created an entire global generation of people who were raised within a context with the very meaning of communication, the very meaning of culture, is manipulation.**
>
> **Jaron Lainer**

My choice is to avoid social media completely. I deleted it on my 30th birthday and never looked back — and I have also never been happier. I focus on real life, not the representation of my life that I want to show others online. And this is where the ability to hold conversations becomes very interesting because, and I do not have any scientific basis for saying this, I believe that having regular meaningful conversations can somewhat mitigate the negative effect of our use of technology, including smartphones and social media.

## The Benefits of Conversation.

### *Changing* your mind.

The ability to hold a conversation profoundly changes us. In fact, *anything* we repeatedly do changes us. This brings to mind the following:

> **We are what we repeatedly do.** **Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit.**
>
> Aristotle.

This is because of the way that our brains work, which is fascinating. The brain is a highly adaptable organ that continually rewires itself based on our experiences, behaviours, and environment. When we learn a new skill, acquire knowledge in an unfamiliar domain, or adjust to a major life transition, our brains form new neural connections and pathways to adapt. Over time, these connections are strengthened through repetition and become adept mental shortcuts. While this neuroplasticity allows us to grow and progress throughout life, it also means our brains are highly sensitive to both positive and negative influences in our environments. The activities we engage in, the information we consume, and the ways we live shape our mental experiences and physical brain structures. **The life you lead determines the brain you build.**

As we become better conversationalists, this creates a positive cycle that reinforces itself and has spillover effects across our entire lives. Being a great conversationalist literally changes your mind. The benefits of conversation are so numerous that I actually struggled to catalogue and categorize them. I have now reached the conclusion, and I am sure you will too once you see the spectrum of benefits, that conversation is a skill that needs to be placed on the same level as learning, meditation, patience, and critical thinking.

What is also interesting is that a lot of these benefits can flow to the people that you converse with. You can help others gain diverse and deeper perspective, to discuss and consider uncomfortable ideas, and even build their confidence by practicing with you.

These benefits can acruee to others even without their knowledge. We all have mirror neurons in our brain that activate when we see someone else perform an action or demonstrate an emotion. When you engage in an uplifting or inspiring conversation with someone, their mirror neurons are activated, and they unconsciously pick up on the positive feelings and energy, which then releases similar chemicals in their own brain. Even over the phone or video, we detect subtle cues in tone of voice, speed of speech, and visual cues that our mirror neurons respond to, allowing for emotional contagion, both good and bad. So when you make an effort to be fully present, listen well, share authentically and bring a positive, compassionate mindset to your conversations, you not only derive benefits yourself but also elevate others through this process of unconscious neural synchrony and mimicked biological feedback loops.

Your positive social exchanges set off a cascade of rewards in the speaker's brain that makes them feel good when interacting with you, even without knowing precisely why. At a biological level, this is because fundamental pleasure centers in the brain activate to regulate the release of dopamine, oxytocin and serotonin during and after the conversation — neurotransmitters responsible for feelings of enjoyment, trust, empathy and well-being. The more upbeat, engaging or profound the discussion, the more pronounced these effects tend to be. But in short, being a positive force for others through caring, approachable dialogue has rippling impacts that spread to those you converse with, both consciously through the meaning gleaned from such exchanges, as well as unconsciously through the biological reactions set in motion beneath awareness as mirror neurons drink in the emotional qualities of each interaction. This interconnectedness makes each conversation an opportunity to uplift — or diminish — well beyond the immediate moment or what is spoken aloud. Our words matter deeply.

Finally, we also have to remember that conversation is also non-negotiable. Unless you decide to go and live in a monastery or become a hermit, you will, be sheer necessity, need to speak with other human beings. We are social creatures dependent on interaction with others for survival, growth, and fulfillment. While technology provides opportunities to limit certain types of interpersonal exchange, it cannot replace the relationships and real life social interaction that give life deep meaning. Conversation remains our primary mode of building familiarity and trust, sharing knowledge, grappling with differences, sparking new ideas, navigating challenges together, and forming the bonds that sustain well-being. Every stage of life and domain of society necessitates communication. From our first infant cries to the exchanges that shape parent-child attachment, learning language as toddlers, navigating friendships in childhood and adolescence, courting romantic partners, interviewing for jobs, working on collaborative projects, strengthening lifelong relationships, or sharing wisdom as elders — life progresses through conversation.

So the benefits are both for you personally and also for the people you converse with, and likely the ripple effects continue across society. But let's narrow our discussion of the benefits of great conversations to ourselves. We can broadly subdivide the benefits into the following areas:

-   Mental & Physical Health Benefits
-   Work & Career Benefits
-   Social & Relationship Benefits
-   Practical & Self-Improvement Benefits

There is also an entire topic of benefits related to negotiations, but I think that this is such an interesting topic that it warrants an essay in of itself, and so I won't cover the link between conversation and negotiation in this essay.

### Mental & Physical Health Benefits

I was quite surprised when I found out that there were so many health-related benefits to having regular meaningful conversations. It doesn't feel that having conversations should be as important as exercise and diet — but it is.

As we become better conversationalists, we learn more about others and are exposed to new ideas, or at least to familiar ideas but with deeper insight.

As we continue to develop our conversational skills, we become more empathetic and understanding of the people around us. This growth in empathy enables us to form stronger connections and relationships with others, which can lead to a more fulfilling life.

In addition to enhancing our relationships, being a good conversationalist can also open up opportunities for personal and professional growth. Engaging in meaningful conversations can expand our knowledge, challenge our beliefs, and develop critical thinking skills. This intellectual growth benefits us as individuals and contributes to the betterment of society as a whole.
Moreover, being an effective conversationalist can help us navigate difficult situations and resolve intrapersonal conflicts more effectively. Understanding and respecting different perspectives allows us to find common ground and work towards solutions that benefit all parties involved.

### Asking Great Questions.

Let's discuss *what* great questions are, and we can tackle *how* to ask great questions later on.

Great questions are the ones that make people think. These questions they have not heard before and elicit longer and more thoughtful responses instead of the typical yes/no response. You want to understand the details, the motivations, and their unique insights.

Let's look at some examples.

Bad question: *"Do you like your job?"*

This question can easily be answered with a simple yes or no, and it doesn't encourage the person to share more about their experiences or thoughts.

Good question: *"What aspects of your job do you find most challenging?"*

This question encourages the person to think more deeply about their job and share their experiences, thoughts, and feelings. It opens up the conversation for a more meaningful and engaging discussion, allowing both parties to learn more about each other and connect on a deeper level.

Once they have told you about the aspects of their work that they find most challenging, you can ask follow-up questions about how they would do things differently if they had 100% control over their work, what they think about the future of their industry, and so on.

This kind of technique can encourage the conversation to move from a lower level (i.e. gossip or personal experience) to a higher level, focusing on future aspirations, wider societal change, and even the philosophy of a specific subject.

In many cases, I find that the person I am talking to vocalizes specific thoughts about their life or work for the first time. Because it is so rare to come across people who truly listen, you may likely be the first person to ask deep and pointed questions about a particular subject to a person. They may have had certain thoughts before but have never had a chance to discuss them with anyone. This is great because it turns the conversation with you into a far more memorable and meaningful experience than it otherwise would be.
